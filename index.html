Betfair Romania Development

Getting more customers to opt in to a rewards proposition for a large online entertainment operator

Role Product OwnerTeam PM, PO, designer, engineeringTimeline 6 monthsNorth Star Increase opt-in rate
Challenge

An unclear opt-in and opt-out journey, and rewards that didn't feel real.

What we did

Research showed believability was the bigger problem, so we fixed it first, then the opt-in journey.

Outcome+21%

relative increase in opt-in rate in the first 3 months

Read the full storyHide the full story · context, problem, discovery, decision, result, reflection
Context

The proposition gives customers the chance to win rewards. To take part, they first need to opt in. It is live across three brands, so the team's focus moved from launching it to growing participation.

Problem

Customer feedback and research pointed to two problems holding back opt-in:

  • An unclear opt-in and opt-out journey.
  • Rewards that didn't feel real. Awareness and believability were low.
Discovery

We defined the problems from two sources: customer service contacts and a customer research survey.

  • Customer contacts showed recurring themes: confusion about being opted in, and questions about how the proposition works.
  • Belief in the proposition was the biggest blocker.

I synthesised the customer contacts and research into the problem statements, which became the basis for prioritising against our North Star.

Decision

We initially assumed the opt-in and opt-out journey was the biggest issue. The customer research report showed that it was a real problem, but believability was a bigger one, so we prioritised it first.

We also chose to start with optimisations to the existing journey, such as improving how rewards are merchandised, and to leave new features for a later stage. Within the optimisations we worked in this order:

  • Believability: improved messaging and experience so customers feel genuinely rewarded and believe in the proposition.
  • Opt-in and opt-out: a smooth experience that lets customers opt in when their motivation is highest, and opt out just as easily.
Result

+21% relative increase in opt-in rate in the first 3 months. Two experiments are scheduled to test the proposition banner, comparing which information is most relevant to customers.

Reflection

Our first assumption about the biggest problem turned out to be wrong. Checking it against customer research before committing changed our priorities, and it is the reason the opt-in rate is moving.

Strategy note · the next 3 months of this case study

How we plan to reach the opt-in target

Research showed that low belief in the proposition is the biggest blocker to opting in. The next phase builds on that, with changes based on customer feedback and agreed with stakeholders.

Read the full planHide the full plan · planned changes, success metrics, risks, sequence
Ship directly

Show the total rewards given

Add the overall number of rewards given in relevant, visible places across the proposition.

Proof that rewards are really paid out makes the proposition more believable. It needs no experiment because it directly addresses the main blocker.

Experiment 1

Explain how it works on the banner

For opted-out customers, the banner shows more information about the rewards and how the proposition works.

Customers currently have a limited understanding of the mechanics. Educational content will build stronger belief and anticipation, improving sentiment and opt-in confidence.

Experiment 2

Show rewards being given in real time

The banner adds real-time information about the rewards being given.

Seeing rewards won as they happen makes the proposition feel real and increases belief, measured the same way as experiment 1.

Success metrics
  • Primary: opt-in rate to the proposition
  • Leading: activity on the banner among opted-out customers
  • Supporting: sentiment and belief in the proposition, from customer research
  • Supporting: fewer customer contacts asking how the proposition works
  • Guardrail: opt-out rate and complaints stay stable
Risks and how we handle them
  • Overlapping changes. The rewards counter goes live first, so the experiments run against a new baseline.
  • A busier banner. More information could reduce clarity, so banner activity is tracked alongside opt-in.
  • Credibility of numbers. Rewards figures must be accurate, current and compliant with marketing rules.
  • Time. Each experiment needs enough traffic for a clear result within the remaining months.
Sequence
  1. Step 1Launch the rewards counter and set a new baseline
  2. Step 2Run experiment 1: educational banner
  3. Step 3Run experiment 2: real-time rewards
  4. Step 4Roll out the winning banner
Suvoda

Bringing two products together in a patient app for clinical trials, through a company merger

Role Product OwnerTeam 10 → 25 people during the mergerFramework SAFe, quarterly releasesTimeline 6 months
Challenge

Two products and two roadmaps had to become one, under clinical trial regulations, without missing a release.

What I did

Merged the roadmaps into one backlog, mapped dependencies for the payment integration, and aligned three teams and ten stakeholders.

OutcomeOn time

quarterly releases throughout the merger, with ~30% less refinement prep time

Read the full storyHide the full story · context, problem, approach, result, reflection
Context

Suvoda builds software for clinical trials. I was the Product Owner for its mobile app used by patients enrolled in clinical studies, covering visit scheduling, notifications for assessments, and payments.

During this time Suvoda merged with a company that had its own payment solution.

Problem

Two products, two roadmaps and two sets of priorities had to become one, while quarterly releases stayed on schedule. The merged company's payment back end had to work with our app's existing interface, and clinical trial regulations shaped what we could change and how.

Approach
  • One roadmap. Two product roadmaps merged into one prioritised backlog, working through conflicting priorities between the products.
  • Integration. I defined requirements and mapped cross-team dependencies to bring the new payment back end into the existing product. We adjusted existing features and designed new ones to fit the integration.
  • Alignment. I coordinated three dependent teams and ten key stakeholders across the US and Eastern Europe while my own team grew from 10 to 25 people.
  • Faster refinement. I used AI-assisted workflows to draft specs and user stories, which left more time for alignment.
  • Balance. Working with UX, engineering and QA, I balanced regulatory constraints with user needs and commercial priorities.
Result

Quarterly releases stayed on time throughout the merger, with about 30% less prep time for refinement sessions. Clear requirements and mapped dependencies unblocked engineering and prevented rework during a period of significant organisational change.

Reflection

Two innovative products can be stronger together when they share the same goal: making things easier for patients. Getting there meant making trade-offs and staying open to change and adjustment along the way.
